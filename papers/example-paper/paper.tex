\section{Introduction \& Motivation}
An editor choosing next week's technology coverage may see a discussion thread
about ``small local models,'' a search query for ``on-device AI,'' and a news
headline about edge inference. Each source exposes a different slice of
interest. A single-platform trending list misses this connection, whereas a
count of every mention tends to favor established, high-volume topics. The
practical question is whether several weak signals can be combined into a
short, explainable list of emerging themes.

The intended users are journalists, content teams, and product researchers
who need candidates for human investigation, not an automatic forecast. A
useful radar should answer three questions for each candidate: what changed,
where it changed, and which examples support the label. It should also avoid
treating ten nearly identical posts as ten independent confirmations. The
project therefore separates collection, semantic grouping, scoring, and
presentation. Its contribution is a transparent design for combining these
steps and a small worked evaluation that shows how to inspect both successes
and failures.

This paper describes a teaching prototype. It uses a hand-constructed,
synthetic four-week scenario rather than live platform data, so the reported
numbers demonstrate an evaluation procedure rather than a measured market
trend. That boundary is important: a plausible pipeline can be tested for
internal consistency with a toy sample, but evidence of real-world utility
would require a larger, time-separated corpus and independent reviewers.

\section{State of the Art}
Simple popularity lists rank a post or query by its current count. They are
fast and easy to audit, but each platform has its own audience and units;
raw counts from a discussion forum and a search index are not directly
comparable. Keyword matching is similarly transparent, yet it splits
paraphrases such as ``small local model'' and ``on-device LLM.'' A manually
maintained alias list can repair known pairs, but it becomes brittle as new
phrases appear. These approaches remain valuable baselines because their
failure modes are visible.

Probabilistic topic models such as latent Dirichlet allocation represent
documents as mixtures of word-based topics \autocite{blei2003lda}. They can
summarize a corpus, but short posts and headlines provide little word evidence
per item, and a topic distribution does not by itself explain whether
cross-source attention is increasing. Sentence embeddings offer a more direct
way to compare short, differently worded texts. Sentence-BERT was developed
for efficient semantic similarity using fixed-size vectors
\autocite{reimers2019sbert}, building on transformer representations
\autocite{vaswani2017attention}. Embeddings can also merge texts that are
related but should remain distinct, so cluster membership needs inspection.

We choose embedding-based grouping for short phrases, retain source-level
counts for an auditable rank, and treat a generated description as a draft
only. This is a narrower task than predicting future demand. It asks whether
cross-platform evidence can produce better review candidates than a
single-count baseline within a fixed observation window.

\section{Method}
\textbf{Inputs and collection.} The illustrative scenario contains four
weekly snapshots from three streams: discussion titles, search-interest
terms, and news headlines. The discussion stream resembles a public forum
such as Reddit; the search stream resembles a Google Trends export. No API
calls, scraped pages, or personal records are needed to build this paper.
Each record contains a week, source, text or query phrase, and an aggregate
activity value. The prototype would store source identifiers and permitted
links for review, but the worked example uses only synthetic aggregates.
Discussion reposts with identical normalized titles count once within a week.
Weekly snapshots use one agreed time zone and close at the same hour.
Otherwise a Sunday post could be counted in different weeks by different
collectors and create artificial growth. Missing source exports are recorded
as missing, not silently interpreted as zero interest. A live collector would
also log failed requests, record counts before and after deduplication, and
the time of each source's last successful update. Those fields make a sudden
increase easier to distinguish from a repaired data feed. We prefer an
explicit batch job to a continuous stream: weekly ranking is sufficient for
an editorial meeting and lets reviewers freeze each week's inputs.
If an entire source export fails, that week is withheld from ranking; the
zero used later in the activity mean denotes an available source with no
matching items for a particular topic.

\textbf{Normalization and grouping.} We lowercase text, remove URLs and
tracking strings, keep meaningful technical tokens such as ``RAG,'' and map
obvious spelling variants to one form. Search phrases, titles, and headlines
are embedded with a frozen Sentence-BERT encoder: its sentence vectors let us
compare short paraphrases without training on the toy records. Cosine similarity is
compared with a fixed threshold of 0.78; an item joins the nearest existing
cluster above the threshold or starts a new cluster. This threshold is an
illustrative design choice, not a tuned optimum. A nearest-centroid rule is
simple enough to inspect, while a fully trained topic model would be hard to
justify with only a few weeks of data. Figure~\ref{fig:radar} summarizes the
data flow. The cluster's displayed name is selected from its most frequent
short phrase, then checked by a reviewer.
The nearest-centroid rule can assign a new weekly item without recomputing
all earlier groups. It also introduces an order effect: a vague phrase may
shift a centroid and attract unrelated later phrases. To limit this, the
prototype compares each candidate with both the centroid and a representative
member and flags disagreement for review. A production version should
compare this rule with density-based clustering on a held-out set of
human-grouped phrases. We do not fine-tune the encoder on the toy records;
that would make the evaluation circular. The chosen sentence encoder is a
general starting point, not a claim that one model is optimal for every
language or technical vocabulary.

\textbf{Comparable signals.} For each source and week, we scale the observed
activity of a cluster to $[0,1]$ relative to that source's weekly maximum.
This permits a search index and an aggregate post count to contribute without
pretending they share a unit. Let $a_t$ be the mean of the three scaled values
for topic $t$ in the current week, including zero for absent sources. Let
$g_t=\min(1,\max(0,(a_t-a'_t)/(a'_t+0.1)))$, where $a'_t$ is the previous
week's activity. The constant 0.1 prevents a near-zero denominator from
producing an extreme growth value. Let $d_t$ be the fraction of sources with
nonzero activity. The ranking score is
\begin{equation}
  S_t=0.45g_t+0.35a_t+0.20d_t.
  \label{eq:radar-score}
\end{equation}
The weights in Equation~\ref{eq:radar-score} favor change while retaining
current activity and corroboration. They were set before the illustrative
comparison; fitting them to ten labels would invite overfitting. For example,
$g_t=0.8$, $a_t=0.6$, and two active sources give $S_t\approx0.70$. The
dashboard shows all three terms beside the final score so a reviewer can
identify why a candidate moved upward.
The terms express distinct design goals. Activity prevents a one-off mention
from topping the list solely through a growth ratio. Growth lets a new topic
compete with a familiar but stable one. Diversity rewards a theme seen in
more than one kind of source. The weights are an editorial policy that users
should be able to challenge. A ranker trained on historical click-through
could optimize engagement instead, but it might learn prior coverage patterns
and hide why a theme was promoted. For this prototype, a visible weighted
score is easier to justify than an opaque learned ranker.

Normalizing by each source's weekly maximum is simple, but an exceptionally
popular item compresses the scaled values of every other item that week. A
percentile transform would resist spikes, although it would be harder to
explain to an editor reading one topic's record. The dashboard retains raw
aggregates and the weekly scaling maximum. Reviewers can see whether a score
changed because the topic grew or because another topic dominated its source.
This trace is as important as the final rank.

\textbf{Presentation.} A short language-model prompt can turn the top three
representative snippets into a draft cluster description. The prompt asks for
a neutral summary with source links and forbids claims about future growth.
The score never uses generated text, and a reviewer must check each sentence
against its linked snippets before publication. This separates ranking from
wording: an incorrect summary cannot silently change the order of topics.
The interface also displays the four-week activity trace, source breakdown,
and cluster members so a generic label can be challenged.
For a summary draft, the prompt receives a fixed set of representative
snippets, their source names, and dates. It cannot browse or fill gaps with
unstated background knowledge. A statement without support in a supplied
snippet should be removed. The draft also states when a cluster has only one
source, avoiding language such as ``widely discussed'' for a single-feed
spike. A reviewer can relabel or split a cluster before publication; the
revised score is calculated from the new membership rather than edited by
hand.

\section{Results \& Evaluation}
We use a small worked exercise to demonstrate how the radar should be
evaluated. Ten synthetic candidate clusters were created with four weekly
aggregate snapshots and short example texts. One author assigned a binary
``useful review candidate'' label after reading the texts, before comparing
the ranking outputs. Five candidates were labeled relevant. The baseline
orders candidates by current total activity alone. A second comparison adds
growth but uses exact phrase groups; the full radar uses semantic clusters
and all three score terms. Precision at five, the fraction of the first five
candidates labeled relevant, is suitable for a reviewer who can inspect only
a short list \autocite{manning2008ir}.
The ten candidates include five positive and five negative labels. The
``useful review candidate'' label is based on specificity of the theme and
plausible recent change in its short texts, not on the radar score itself.
Source values and labels were fixed before generating the ranking lists.
This avoids changing the answer key after seeing which method wins, although
a sample designed by the authors can still favor their hypothesis. All
comparisons use the same candidates and weekly window; only grouping and
ordering differ.

Table~\ref{tab:radar-results} gives the resulting top-five counts. The full
radar moves one additional relevant candidate into the review set, a
difference of one item rather than proof of superior forecasting. The growth
baseline does not improve on volume in this sample because two new phrases
for the same theme remain separate. Semantic grouping puts them in one
cluster and exposes activity on two streams. The exact phrase method is
therefore a useful ablation: it tests the contribution of grouping separately
from the weighting of signals.
Because there are five relevant candidates, the full radar's four true
positives in its top five imply one false positive and one missed relevant
candidate. The volume baseline has three true positives, two false positives,
and two misses. The difference saves one of five review slots in this
constructed case. No significance test would be meaningful on ten examples,
and the ranking of the remaining five candidates matters if a reviewer has
more time. We use a top-five cutoff because it models a specific workflow,
not because that cutoff is universally best.

A worked score illustrates what the dashboard explains. The ``on-device
models'' cluster has scaled current activity $a_t=0.60$, growth $g_t=0.80$,
and activity on two of three sources, yielding $S_t\approx0.70$. The
discussion snippets say ``small local model,'' while the search phrase says
``on-device LLM.'' A volume-only list treats those separately and ranks a
long-established high-count topic above them. The example supports the
mechanism of the proposed score; it does not show that the cluster will
remain important next month.

We also inspect a failure case. Two headlines containing ``AI agent'' refer
to different applications, yet their embeddings fall above the chosen
similarity threshold. The merged cluster receives an inflated activity value
and a misleading generated label. Looking at its member snippets reveals
the error immediately. A review action that splits the cluster and recomputes
the score is preferable to publishing an unqualified rank. This case shows
why qualitative error analysis belongs beside a headline metric.
The split action has a visible consequence. Removing the unrelated headline
lowers that cluster's activity and may reduce its diversity term if the
headline was its only news item. A robust interface should show the score
before and after review, not merely hide the record. The reverse error is
also possible: ``edge inference'' and ``on-device AI'' may fall below the
threshold and appear as separate candidates, wasting two review slots.
Near-neighbor suggestions to an editor are a modest way to catch both errors
without pretending that one threshold is universally correct.

Two sensitivity checks clarify what the toy comparison does and does not
support. Lowering the similarity threshold would merge more phrases, which
may recover paraphrases but also increase the ``AI agent'' error. Raising it
would reduce false merges while making the on-device theme less visible. We
therefore should not select the threshold solely because it maximizes
Precision@5 on this tiny sample. Removing the diversity weight would allow a
single-source spike to rise more easily. These directional checks follow
from the method; they are not additional measured gains. A real ablation
would tune thresholds and weights on development weeks and report one
untouched test period.

The score calculation itself needs tests. For the worked on-device candidate,
$0.45(0.80)+0.35(0.60)+0.20(2/3)$ is approximately $0.70$, matching the
dashboard value. A zero-activity candidate must receive zero for activity
and diversity, and a topic with unchanged activity must receive zero growth.
These boundary checks prevent presentation errors from being mistaken for
modeling effects. When an entire source is unavailable, the interface marks
the week incomplete instead of quietly computing a three-source score from
two feeds. Small checks like these make an explainable ranker inspectable.

The exercise deliberately omits latency and cost figures because no deployed
collector or model service was measured. A real evaluation would report
collection coverage, duplicate rate, clustering agreement with independent
labels, ranking precision on future weeks, and processing time on specified
hardware. Reporting numbers without that setting would make the example look
more certain than its evidence allows.
Generated descriptions need a separate evaluation from ranking. Independent
reviewers could check whether every claim is supported by linked snippets,
whether the time window is correct, and whether contradictory evidence is
mentioned. A fluent summary is not necessarily faithful. We report no
summary accuracy score because a few hand-written snippets cannot support a
general estimate. The qualitative failure above demonstrates the required
inspection step, not a model error rate.

\section{Discussion \& Limitations}
The apparent gain in Table~\ref{tab:radar-results} is one label among ten
synthetic candidates. It is sensitive to the author who supplied both the
cases and the labels; the relevance criterion may also favor cross-source
signals by construction. Independent annotation, a preselected test period,
and confidence intervals across weeks are necessary before claiming an
operational advantage. The fixed similarity threshold can merge distinct
topics or split paraphrases, particularly when posts are short or multilingual.
Manual review remains part of the method rather than an afterthought.
There is also ambiguity in ``trend.'' The score detects a recent change in
observed attention, which may come from a launch, a controversy, or a brief
media cycle. It does not establish technical progress, commercial demand, or
the future direction of a field. A reviewer should record why a candidate is
worth following. The dashboard should retain dismissed candidates and their
reasons; otherwise a later evaluation would see only accepted examples and
overstate quality.

Platform coverage is another limit. Search indices, news feeds, and forum
activity each reflect different populations and editorial choices. API
restrictions, bot activity, language imbalance, and changing moderation can
move counts without a corresponding change in public interest. Weekly
normalization removes units but also obscures absolute scale. The radar should
therefore display original aggregates and never present its score as a direct
measure of public opinion. If live sources are added, collection must respect
their terms and minimize personal data; retaining only aggregate counts and
permitted source links reduces, but does not eliminate, privacy concerns.

Finally, a language model can invent causal explanations for a rising
cluster. Grounded snippets and mandatory human approval constrain that risk,
but do not guarantee accuracy. A practical next iteration would compare
summary drafts with source-verified human descriptions and log corrections.
The score itself should be stress-tested under missing-source and sudden
volume-spike scenarios before anyone uses it to prioritize coverage.
Human review has a cost: inspecting snippets and correcting clusters takes
time, and editors may apply inconsistent standards across weeks. A deployment
study should record review time and agreement between editors alongside
Precision@5. If one extra relevant candidate requires substantially more
review effort, its practical value may be limited. This is another reason to
expose intermediate evidence instead of only a ranking metric.

\section{Conclusion}
This example develops an inspectable pipeline for identifying candidate AI
trends across discussion, search, and news streams. Semantic grouping,
normalized activity, growth, and source diversity yield a review list with
traceable evidence. The illustrative top-five comparison and error inspection
show how to evaluate such a system without treating a toy result as a live
forecast. The next step is a time-separated study on legally accessible
aggregate data with independent labels and measured processing costs.
